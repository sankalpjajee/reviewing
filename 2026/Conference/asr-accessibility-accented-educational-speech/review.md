# Consolidated Review

**Paper:** "The Words Are There, but the Lesson Is Missing: Evaluating ASR Accessibility in Accented Educational Speech" (anonymous submission, AAAI-style format, 9 pages; the Appendix the text refers to is not included in the reviewed file).

**How this review was produced.** Twelve independent reviewer agents each read the full paper and page images through one lens (methodology, statistics, internal consistency, ASR, LLM-QA evaluation, human study, accessibility/education, novelty, ethics/reproducibility, writing, plus a designated skeptic and a designated advocate). They produced 236 raw findings, which were consolidated here. The 96 findings from the methodology, statistics, consistency, ASR, and part of the LLM-QA lenses were then each checked by two independent adversarial verifiers (one trying to refute the finding against the paper text and by recomputation, one judging materiality). None was fully refuted; one was dropped (a supposed rounding discrepancy in Table 4 that disappears with unrounded inputs) and about thirty were narrowed or corrected, and those corrections are incorporated below (see Section 9). The remaining lenses' findings were not adversarially verified because of an account usage limit, but every number quoted anywhere in this review was recomputed from the paper's own tables (Section 5). Findings that depend on reading tiny bar labels in Figure 4 are flagged as such.

---

## 1. Verdict

The paper proposes a sensible idea: judge an ASR transcript of a lecture not by word error rate (WER) but by whether a reader can still answer factual questions about the lesson's key terms, then ask whether accented (L2) instructors are disadvantaged. The paired design (same items answered from a gold window and from an ASR window, McNemar on discordant pairs, Cohen's h alongside p-values) is the right structure, the two corpora are real educational speech, and the limitations section is candid.

However, the manuscript does not currently support its own headline claims. The abstract and conclusion assert QA gaps "across recording topics" and "in knowledge-intensive material" and "substantial differences among QA LLMs", but no table or figure reports QA accuracy by topic or knowledge intensity, and the three LLMs differ by under 2 points in the reference condition. The central equity claim in the title is never tested: QA accuracy is never split by L1/L2 speaker. The body text reports reference QA accuracies (99.3/98.3/97.9%) that contradict the paper's own Figure 4 (95.4/94.0/93.5%) and Figure 1 (94.3%). Table 3 labels tests as N=1,107 items when the counts are pooled across three LLMs (3,321 pairs). Several interpretive sentences are contradicted by the paper's own numbers (BWER is *below* WER for every Whisper row; the relative L1/L2 gap is the same ~1.4x for the best Whisper and the worst wav2vec2 model). The Whisper-Large results are, by the authors' own account, a long-form decoding artifact, yet they are used as model performance throughout. The human study's entire protocol is deferred to a missing appendix, and the "Ethical Concerns" section contains no ethics content despite biographical profiling of identifiable instructors.

**Recommendation: reject in current form; encourage resubmission after major revision.** Score 4/10, confidence 4/5. The underlying study is worth publishing once the text matches the data, the item-selection and pooling issues are handled, and the L1/L2 QA analysis the title promises is actually run.

## 2. What the paper does

- **Framework.** Stage 1: from a gold reference transcript, an LLM (Gemini) identifies "knowledge-intensive" keywords and writes one four-option multiple-choice question per keyword, using only the keyword and a +/-100-word window; items pass a leak filter and an answerability check by two LLM judges (GPT4o, Llama3). Stage 2: three QA LLMs (GPT4o, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) answer each item twice, once with the reference window and once with the corresponding ASR window. The reference-minus-ASR accuracy gap is the "accessibility" measure.
- **ASR systems.** Four Whisper checkpoints (S, M, L, "L-t") and three wav2vec2 checkpoints (B, L, "L-s"), all off-the-shelf from Hugging Face; Whisper decoded with language fixed to English.
- **Data.** EdX: 1,380 lecture videos from 27 instructors in 5 subject areas (343 L1 / 1,037 L2 videos), 148 excluded, 1,232 used. M3AV: 123 academic talks from 3 venues (NIH, IPP, MLS; 31 L1 / 92 L2). L1/L2 assigned by biographical research on instructors (EdX) and by name/affiliation hypothesis partly verified via OpenAlex (M3AV). 1,113 "knowledge-error keywords" yielded 1,107 questions, 813 of them "ground-truth-verified".
- **Metrics.** CER, WER, and BWER (keyword error rate: slide-OCR terms for M3AV, all non-function words for EdX); QA accuracy; McNemar exact test on discordant pairs; Cohen's h.
- **Results.** Table 2: Whisper-S/M best (EdX WER 0.075/0.074), Whisper-L worst of Whisper (0.197) attributed to hallucination/repetition on long audio; wav2vec2 0.164-0.253. Figure 2: L2 WER higher than L1 for every system (e.g. Whisper-M 0.080 vs 0.056). Figure 4: QA accuracy drops from ~94.3% (reference, mean of three LLMs) to ~90.6% with Whisper-M transcripts (3.7 pt); larger drops with wav2vec2. Table 3: 160 "toward" (reference right, ASR wrong) vs 37 "away" discordant pairs, p = 9.9e-20, h = 0.140; substitution errors dominate. Table 4: on 300 items reformatted as open-ended, drops grow to 10.3-14.0 pt (h 0.24-0.34). Human study (235 clips of <= 25 words): L2 speakers rated more accented (4.3 vs 2.6 on 9-point scale); ratings correlate with ASR BWER (Spearman 0.15-0.52); Table 7 splits 194 clips by whether QA was 100% correct and reports higher L2 share and accentedness in the imperfect group.

## 3. What holds up under scrutiny

- **Statistical arithmetic.** All seven reported p-values (Table 3 and the open-ended test) reproduce exactly as one-sided exact binomial tests on the discordant counts (160/37 -> 9.94e-20; 137/29 -> 2.74e-18; 23/8 -> 5.34e-3; 89/12 -> 5.41e-16; 75/6 -> 1.46e-16; 14/6 -> 5.77e-2; 140/33 -> 3.43e-17). Table 4's Cohen's h values reproduce (0.240, 0.252, 0.338). Substitution + deletion counts sum to the "All" rows.
- **Count bookkeeping.** 343+1,037 = 1,380; 31+92 = 123; 1,380-148 = 1,232; 1,232+123 = 1,355; 457+656 = 1,113; Table 5 n's sum to 235 under both groupings; Table 7 rows sum to 194; the 75/25 L2/L1 split holds for both corpora (75.1%, 74.8%).
- **Figure-to-table consistency.** Figure 2's L1/L2 WERs, weighted 25/75, reproduce Table 2's overall WER for every system (e.g. Whisper-S 0.25*0.058 + 0.75*0.081 = 0.075). Figure 1's 94.3%/90.6%/3.7 pt/p=9.9e-20 equal the three-LLM means of Figure 4's reference and Whisper-M bars and Table 3's full-set row.
- **Direction of the main effects.** L2 WER exceeds L1 WER for all seven systems. ASR-conditioned QA accuracy is below reference accuracy for all 21 LLM x ASR combinations. Discordant pairs run strongly toward "reference right, ASR wrong". Substitution errors on keywords account for most of the loss. The open-ended format produces a larger absolute drop than multiple choice.
- **Design choices that are better than typical.** Questions are generated from the reference, not the ASR output; the generator is never an answerer; the open-ended variant changes only the answer format; effect sizes are reported next to p-values and the multiple-choice effect is honestly called "modest"; the partial verifier/answerer overlap is disclosed rather than hidden; decoding is deterministic where possible; the paper flags that the larger Whisper models fail on long audio rather than reporting the anomaly uncritically.

## 4. Weaknesses, ranked

### Critical

**C1. Headline claims about topics, knowledge intensity, and LLM differences have no supporting result.** (Abstract; Conclusions p.7; Methodology p.2.) The abstract says results "show substantial differences among QA LLMs and across recording topics, with the largest performance gaps occurring in knowledge-intensive material"; the conclusion repeats "downstream QA models differ substantially across topics". No table or figure reports QA accuracy by topic, venue, or the lower/higher knowledge-intensity label defined on p.2 (which is never used again). Figure 3 is Whisper-M *ASR* error by domain, not QA. The "substantial differences among QA LLMs" clause is weakly supported at best: reference accuracies span 1.9 pt (0.954/0.940/0.935) and the text itself calls them "very similar"; ASR-condition spreads reach 3.4-6.2 pt and the open-ended reference spread is 7 pt, but no between-model test is reported although "statistical tests for important differences among LLMs" are promised (p.4). *Fix:* add a QA-by-topic and QA-by-knowledge-intensity table with per-cell n and tests, or remove these sentences from the abstract and conclusion.

**C2. The equity claim in the title is never tested: QA accuracy is not disaggregated by L1/L2.** (Figure 4, Tables 3-4, Conclusions.) Every video carries an L1/L2 label, yet QA accuracy, the reference-vs-ASR drop, and h are reported only pooled over speakers. ASR BWER is also never split by L1/L2. The only speaker-group-to-QA link is Table 7 (194 human-study clips): L2 share 84% vs 91%, no test reported; on the implied counts (47/56 vs 126/138) a two-sided Fisher exact test gives p = 0.20. The conclusion that errors "make some instructors' knowledge less searchable, reviewable, and usable than others'" is therefore not demonstrated by the paper's data. *Fix:* report per-L1/L2 (and per accentedness tertile) QA accuracy, toward/away counts, and h on the full item set; revise title/abstract if the L2 drop is not larger.

**C3. Body text contradicts Figure 4 and Figure 1 on the headline QA number.** (p.5 first paragraph of "QA Accuracy over ASR Transcriptions"; Figure 4 p.5; Figure 1 p.2.) Text: GPT4o 99.3%, Llama3 98.3%, Qwen2.5 97.9% in the reference condition, attributed to Figure 4. Figure 4's reference bars read 0.954 / 0.940 / 0.935 (mean 0.943 = Figure 1's "Reference 94.3%"). Reconstruction suggests the text's numbers are the GT-verified-subset values: with mean reference 0.985 and net drop 77/2,439, h = 0.189 (reported 0.190), whereas the full-set values give h(0.943, 0.906) = 0.141 (reported 0.140). The paper thus mixes two item sets under one figure reference on its most important number. *Fix:* state which set each number belongs to, correct the text, and add a table of per-model, per-ASR accuracies for both sets.

**C4. Table 3's "N=1,107" and "N=813" tests are pooled across the three QA LLMs; the pooling is undisclosed and violates pair independence.** (Table 3 p.5; "Evaluation Metrics" p.4; open-ended test p.6.) If 160 toward / 37 away were for one model over 1,107 items, the net drop would be 11.1 pt, contradicting Figure 1's 3.7 pt and Figure 4's 2.8/4.8/3.6 pt Whisper-M drops. The counts only make sense over 3 x 1,107 = 3,321 model-item pairs: 123/3,321 = 3.70 pt, and h(0.943, 0.906) = 0.141. The open-ended test (140/33, "n=173") likewise pools 3 x 300 = 900 pairs. The Methods promise "for each QA model" tests that are never shown, and the same corrupted keyword breaks all three models, so the three pairs per item are not independent and the p-values overstate the evidence. *Fix:* report McNemar per model (as Table 4 does) or label N as model-item pairs and use an item-clustered test.

**C5. The item set is conditioned on ASR keyword errors, so the QA gap is not "how much of the recording's knowledge remains accessible".** (p.3 filtering: "keeping only pairs where the ASR output denotes a genuinely different concept"; p.4: "1,113 knowledge-error keywords"; Table 3.) The Methods describe item selection as keeping only reference/ASR pairs that denote different concepts, and Table 3's exhaustive substitution/deletion partition of all 197 discordant pairs (137+23 = 160; 29+8 = 37) is what that design predicts. Consequences: (a) the measured 3.7 pt gap is P(QA fails | ASR corrupted the keyword), an upper-bound conditional quantity, not the abstract's unconditional claim; (b) Figure 4 compares seven ASR systems on an item set selected by one system's failures, and the paper never says which system (Figure 1 suggests Whisper); (c) if, as the design implies, the keyword is corrupted in every item under the selecting system, models still answer ~90.6% of items correctly, which questions the probe's sensitivity and the learner-proxy framing (the Limitations partly concede this). *Fix:* state the item-selecting system; add an unconditional control set of knowledge keywords the ASR transcribed correctly; for cross-system comparison define items per system or use an ASR-independent set.

**C6. Whisper-L / Whisper-L-t results are an acknowledged decoding artifact yet are used as model performance everywhere.** (Table 2 p.4; p.5; Figure 2; Table 6.) The text says the 2.7x higher WER of Whisper-L (0.197 vs 0.074) is "driven by hallucination and repetition failures on long lecture audio rather than a genuine capability deficit". This is the well-known sequential long-form decoding failure, routinely mitigated (VAD/chunked decoding, condition_on_previous_text=False, compression-ratio and log-prob fallbacks), and no decoding configuration, inference library, or chunking strategy is reported. The artifact-laden numbers nonetheless drive the Whisper-L L1/L2 gap in Figure 2 (0.133 vs 0.214, the largest gap of any Whisper model), enter Table 6, and justify choosing Whisper-M as "the best working ASR" for all downstream analyses. Signatures of loops are visible in Table 2 itself: Whisper-L's CER/WER ratio is 0.75 vs 0.51 for Whisper-S/M, and its EdX CER (0.148) exceeds Wav2Vec2-B's (0.111). The checkpoints are also unidentified ("Whisper-L" could be large-v1/v2/v3; "L-t" is never expanded, presumably large-v3-turbo, a distilled checkpoint released after the Radford et al. 2023 paper cited for all four). *Fix:* re-decode with standard long-form safeguards and report the configuration for all systems; otherwise label these rows "default settings, artifact-affected" and exclude them from L1/L2 and correlation claims.

### Major

**M1. Two interpretive claims are contradicted by the paper's own numbers.** (p.5; Table 2; Figure 3.) "Topic-specific vocabulary presents a greater challenge than surrounding function words" and "BWER exposes disparities that WER understates". In Table 2, BWER < WER for all four Whisper checkpoints on both corpora (Whisper-M: 0.064 vs 0.074 EdX, 0.053 vs 0.077 M3AV); in Figure 3, Whisper-M BWER <= WER in all eight domains (e.g. NIH 0.061 vs 0.083, IPP 0.039 vs 0.067). BWER exceeds WER only for the wav2vec2 family (e.g. Wav2Vec2-B on M3AV 0.402 vs 0.274), where the claim is well supported; the domain-ranking claim (Engineering, CS, NIH worst) is also supported. Since EdX BWER is "all non-function words", BWER < WER on EdX means content words are recognized *better* than function words. The Figure 3 discussion also names Humanities and Social Sciences as most robust while Science has the lowest EdX error (WER 0.044, BWER 0.043). *Fix:* restrict the claim to wav2vec2 or retract it; report keyword error on the actual triaged keyword set; make EdX BWER comparable to M3AV (see M14).

**M2. "Recent models reduce the L1/L2 accessibility gap" is not supported; the relative disparity is unchanged.** (p.5; Figure 2.) L2/L1 WER ratios from Figure 2: Whisper-S 1.40, Whisper-M 1.43, Whisper-L-t 1.38, Whisper-L 1.61, Wav2Vec2-B 1.40, Wav2Vec2-L 1.39, Wav2Vec2-L-s 1.31. The sentence is literally true for the absolute gap of Whisper-S/M (0.023-0.024 vs 0.079 for Wav2Vec2-B), but the absolute gap shrinks mechanically with lower overall WER, the largest gap on both readings belongs to a Whisper model, and "recent" is undefined for a set of 2020-2022 public checkpoints. The further inference that agreement across systems shows the disparity is "a property of the recordings and speakers rather than an artifact of any one ASR system's training distribution" does not follow: all seven systems are trained predominantly on L1 English, so a shared ordering is exactly what a shared training bias predicts, and the sentence contradicts the Introduction's own attribution to "imbalances in training data". *Fix:* report absolute and relative gaps with speaker-level CIs; rewrite the inference.

**M3. "QA ordering closely mirrors the ASR error-rate ordering from Table 2" fails across families and, apparently, within Whisper; Figure 4's bars are not identified.** (p.5; Figure 4.) Across families: Whisper-L has higher EdX WER (0.197) than Wav2Vec2-L-s (0.164), yet every Whisper bar exceeds every wav2vec2 bar for all three LLMs. Within Whisper (reading the ~3pt bar labels, and assuming bars follow Table 2 order, which the second bar's mean of 0.906 = Figure 1's Whisper-M value supports): GPT4o 0.933 / 0.926 / 0.944 / 0.945, so the two worst-WER checkpoints give the *highest* QA accuracy and the "best working ASR" gives the largest Whisper drop. The likely explanation, that hallucination loops fall outside the +/-100-word windows, is itself an important limitation of window-local QA that the paper does not discuss. *Fix:* label every bar (or add a table), correct the sentence, and discuss that window-local QA is blind to transcript-level failures.

**M4. Verifier/answerer circularity and the unverified "Full" set.** (p.3-4 answerability check; p.4 Datasets; Table 3.) GPT4o and Llama3 confirm that the marked answer is correct given the reference window, then answer the same items from the same window at temperature 0. The GT-verified subset is therefore selected on two of three answerers being right in the reference condition, which pushes their reference accuracy to the ceiling on that subset (the ordering GPT4o > Llama3 > Qwen2.5 also holds on the full set, so the ordering itself is not the evidence; the level is), suppresses "away" pairs (18.8% of discordant pairs on the full set vs 11.9% on the verified set), and makes h rise (0.140 -> 0.190) even though the net drop *falls* (3.7 -> 3.2 pt), because the arcsine transform magnifies gaps near the ceiling. This does not touch the full-set McNemar result, which is the paper's primary evidence. Separately, the Methods say "an item is retained only if both judges independently confirm", yet the headline analyses use 1,107 items of which 294 (26.6%) were not confirmed (both sets are reported side by side, so this is a wording contradiction more than a hidden analysis choice); nothing is said about why so many failed, which judge rejected them, or whether rejections concentrate in dense technical items. The 1,113 -> 1,107 step is plausibly the leak filter. *Fix:* use verifiers disjoint from answerers (or report Qwen2.5 alone on the verified set); make the analysed set consistent with the retention rule; characterize the 294 rejected items.

**M5. The open-ended "unmasking" result is confounded by an unvalidated, stricter judge.** (Table 4; p.6.) The 300 open-ended items are graded by the question generator (Gemini), against its own key and its own unseen distractors, with no human or second-judge agreement. Under this judge the *gold-window* accuracy falls from 94-99% to 77-84%, so 16-23% of "verified" items are unanswerable by construction; the comparison therefore changes the effective item set and relaxes a ceiling at the same time as it changes format. The net directional effect does grow (3.2 -> 11.9 pt pooled), which symmetric judge noise alone cannot produce, so the open-ended result is real evidence of a larger gap; but the reference/ASR odds ratio is ~1.7 under multiple choice and 1.75/1.75/2.27 under open-ended, so in relative terms the two formats show similar effects, and without judge-reliability evidence the size of the "unmasked" effect is not established. The comparison also sets pooled h (0.14-0.19) against per-model h (0.24-0.34); per-model multiple-choice h from Figure 4 is 0.12/0.18/0.13. With n = 300 per model, 95% CIs on the drops are about +/-4.5-5.2 pt, so the per-model ordering in Table 4 is not supported, and only Whisper-M was tested. *Fix:* validate the judge on a human-graded sample; use a judge distinct from the generator; report odds ratios and per-model tests; describe the sampling.

**M6. No closed-book control; the "dependency check" is a prompt instruction, not a measurement.** (p.3-4; Figure 1; Limitations.) Nothing tests whether items are answerable without the window. The Limitations concede "QA models may rely on pretrained knowledge", and Figure 1's showcase item is answerable from general biology (option B is the textbook definition of base excision repair). The leak filter removed at most 6 of 1,113 items, which is plausible only if the generator was already instructed to paraphrase the keyword (the paper does not say), and paraphrased options encode the concept; per-check rejection counts and example items are needed. *Fix:* run all QA models with no context and with a mismatched window on every item; drop or flag closed-book-solvable items; report the gap on the remainder.

**M7. L1/L2 comparison is confounded with venue/topic and ignores speaker clustering; the L1/L2 label itself is undefined and partly name-inferred; M3AV selection is undisclosed.** (p.2-3; Table 1; Figure 2; Table 5.) EdX's 1,232 videos come from 27 instructors, so the L1/L2 contrast is effectively between a handful of people, with no speaker-level aggregation, CIs, or tests anywhere. At corpus level both corpora are ~75% L2 by design and EdX is 91% of videos, so the confound for Figure 2 is within EdX (subject and instructor), which is never cross-tabulated. In the human-study sample the confound is venue: NIH supplies 168 of 235 clips and L2 supplies 196, so the L2 row of Table 5 (4.3 / 3.4 / 0.34) is essentially the NIH row (4.2 / 3.4 / 0.36), and between-topic ranges exceed the L1/L2 gaps. "L1/L2 speaker status is established through biographical research rather than assumed from name or accent alone" sits uneasily with the M3AV procedure two sentences later ("an initial L1 hypothesis is formed from author name origin and affiliation, then verified for a subset"). The text can be read as excluding all unverified speakers, but the verified fraction is unreported, and the M3AV proxies (PhD institution and country, year of US arrival, years of US residence) do not record native language at all. L2 is never defined (how are Indian, Nigerian, British L1 English speakers coded? what is the "reference variety"?). The M3AV sample was built "targeting the same overall 75%/25% L2/L1 composition" from 228+ candidate NIH speakers down to 123 talks, i.e. selected on the study variable by an unstated rule, while p.2 says "all M3AV recordings retained". *Fix:* define L1/L2; report verified vs inferred counts and annotator agreement; give the L1/L2 x topic cross-tab and unique speakers per cell; fit speaker-clustered models; describe the M3AV selection.

**M8. The human study cannot be evaluated or reproduced and its QA link is untested.** (pp.6-7; Tables 5-7.) Not in the main text: number of raters, raters per clip, rater L1/accent familiarity, recruitment, compensation, consent/IRB, scale anchors, replays, blinding, clip sampling rule, inter-rater reliability. Specific problems: (a) "intelligibility" is human-transcription BWER restricted to rare/technical vocabulary and defined differently per corpus, so it largely measures listener domain knowledge and spelling of jargon rather than speaker clarity (L1 speakers miss 28% of keywords; Humanities 0.07 vs NIH 0.36 tracks jargon density), and the "models outperform human listeners" comparison sets full-recording ASR against isolated 25-word clips with no clip-level ASR numbers reported; (b) the 9-point "comprehensibility" is scored so that higher = harder, unstated in the caption; (c) Table 6's caption says "ASR accuracy" but the positive correlations with accentedness only make sense for an error rate, stars are undefined, n and unit are unstated (the star pattern implies ~235 rater-averaged clips treated as independent despite nesting in far fewer speakers), and the text names Whisper-L and Whisper-L-t as the sub-.30 correlations while Whisper-S (0.215) is also below .30; (d) Table 7 dichotomizes the outcome post hoc, reports no test (Fisher p = 0.20 for L2 share), does not say which ASR transcript or how many questions per clip, and "frequently 100% accurate" is imprecise when only 56/194 = 29% of clips were fully correct (per-model accuracy in the rest is 0.84-0.90); question generation also failed for about 45% of L1 clips but only about 12% of L2 clips (L2 share rises from 83% of the 235 rated clips to about 89% of the 194 analysed), which is unexplained; (e) the clip-level QA used a different protocol (<= 25-word clips, no +/-100-word window, and wording that suggests items were generated from ASR output and by "the QA LLMs"), which is at least inconsistent with the Methods' reference-only, Gemini-only generation. The accentedness-ASR-error correlation is also already established in the very work the paper cites (Inceoglu et al. 2023; Hirschi & Kang 2024), and Related Work says prior work found ASR errors "do not align with human perception" while Table 6 reports significant alignment, unreconciled. *Fix:* move the protocol into the main text; report conventional intelligibility; correct Table 5/6 labels and add n/thresholds; model QA correctness as a function of accentedness with topic and speaker effects; describe the clip QA pipeline.

**M9. The accessibility framing is not earned and the title overstates small effects.** (Title; Abstract; p.6; Limitations.) No disabled, Deaf/hard-of-hearing, or multilingual learner is involved; "accessibility" is operationalized as LLM multiple-choice accuracy on a pre-selected window, and the abstract ends by redefining accessibility as "potential for use in LLM-driven downstream tasks". The human study samples hearing listeners rating audio, the inverse of the target population, and so cannot "confirm" the QA proxy as the contributions claim. By the paper's own numbers the best-ASR multiple-choice drop is 2.7-4.7 pt with h 0.14-0.19 (below Cohen's 0.2 "small" anchor, so calling them "small" is generous), while the open-ended h of 0.24-0.34 is called "small-to-medium" (loose rather than wrong, but the rounding runs in the paper's favour both times). The unsourced "99% accuracy" policy hook is never connected to the results, although every system on every speaker group misses it (best word accuracy ~94.4% L1 / 92.0% L2). No implications for institutions or vendors are drawn even though several are available (larger Whisper checkpoints are worse off-the-shelf on long audio; substitution errors dominate; slide-text biasing is an obvious mitigation). *Fix:* either reframe as an ASR-to-LLM robustness benchmark or add a human reading study; describe effect sizes accurately; source the policy claim and relate results to it; add an implications paragraph.

**M10. Ethics gaps.** (p.2-3; p.7 "Limitations and Ethical Concerns".) The study compiles country of origin, native language, years of US residence and "accent/background" for 27 named EdX instructors and forms initial L1 hypotheses from name origin and affiliation for M3AV academics (verified for an unreported subset), with no ethics-board statement, consent, data-protection or retention discussion, and advertises datasets "enriched with speakers' demographics" for release. The human-listener study has no IRB/consent/compensation statement. Dual-use risk (per-instructor "accessibility scores" used against accented instructors) is not discussed. The section titled "Ethical Concerns" contains only methodological limitations. *Fix:* add an ethics statement covering all of the above; release only de-identified or aggregate speaker labels; add a broader-impact paragraph.

**M11. Reproducibility.** Only 2 of 7 ASR checkpoints and 2 of 4 LLMs are identified (no Gemini version; no GPT-4o snapshot); no decoding settings (long-form pipeline, beam, thresholds, wav2vec2 chunking or LM); no verbatim prompts for any of the six LLM roles (possibly in the missing appendix); no description of how the +/-100-word ASR window is located when the anchor keyword is substituted or deleted; no function-word list, "approved equivalent" list, or definition of the "fragmentation/gluing heuristic" and "in-audio explanation check"; no option-shuffling statement (the one shown item has the answer at B; position effects largely cancel in the paired design but matter for absolute accuracies); no seeds, repeats, or variance (generation at temperature 0.3 is a single stochastic draw; GPT-4o at temperature 0 is not deterministic); no data/code/question-set availability statement; EdX licensing described only as "availability under applicable terms". *Fix:* a model table with exact identifiers and parameter counts; full decoding configs; verbatim prompts; the alignment algorithm; release code, items, keyword lists, and de-identified ratings.

**M12. Related work omits the lines of work the paper is closest to, so the gap claims are overstated.** "The quality of transcripts as inputs to the downstream LLMs is underexplored" and "a task-based evaluation is needed" ignore: task-based/QA-based evaluation of ASR transcripts (Munteanu et al. 2006 on webcast lecture transcripts; Favre et al. 2013; Spoken SQuAD and ODSQA; SLUE; ASR-GLUE); question-generation-based evaluation, especially MQAG (Manakul et al. 2023), which is essentially the same generate-MCQ-then-answer-under-two-conditions design; DHH caption-usability metrics that already argue WER is insufficient and weight word importance (Kafle & Huenerfauth 2017, 2019); semantic alternatives to WER (SemDist, Kim et al. 2021); Whisper hallucination literature (Koenecke et al. 2024; WhisperX; Radford et al.'s own long-form heuristics); accent-bias benchmarks of Whisper/wav2vec2 (EdAcc, Sanabria et al. 2023; Chan et al. 2022); the Munro & Derwing (1995) paradigm the human study reproduces without citation; and MCQ-artifact work showing LLMs answer without the passage (Kaushik & Lipton 2018; Balepur et al. 2024). BWER is attributed to the M3AV paper rather than the contextual-biasing literature (Le et al. 2021), and the EdX variant is not BWER. Several Introduction citations do not support the sentences they are attached to (Zheng et al. 2023 and Sidiropoulos et al. 2022 cited for "LLMs ... producing transcripts"). *Fix:* rewrite Related Work around the four areas the paper spans and restate the contribution as applying QA-based evaluation to accented educational speech with keyword-centred items.

**M13. The manuscript is not self-contained and its figures obscure the key comparisons.** "Table 11" is not in the file (presumably an appendix table); "(see Section for details)" is an empty reference; four results are deferred to a missing appendix (verified-subset accuracies, per-ASR tests, human-study design and QC, other correlations), including the "2.7 to 4.7 pt" multiple-choice drops that anchor the open-ended argument. Figure 4 has a truncated y-axis (0.75-1.00), ~3pt rotated labels, and a family-only legend. Figure 1 places "Question Construction" inside the Stage 2 panel, labels the answerer "Llama-3.1-8B" while showing three-LLM pooled numbers, and prints results (94.3%, 90.6%) that appear nowhere in the body. "Two-stage" means ASR+QA in the abstract but generation+QA in the Methods and Figure 1, alongside "four linked steps".

**M14. ASR evaluation choices that bias the family comparison.** The wav2vec2 baselines are LibriSpeech-960h read-speech CTC fine-tunes (letters only, no LM, no digits/hyphens/symbols), so the "two families" contrast mostly measures training-domain mismatch, not "architectures and capability levels", and the described normalization (retaining "content-bearing hyphens, acronyms, and symbols") penalizes wav2vec2 by construction, though plausibly by only a point or two of WER, not enough to change any ordering. EdX BWER ("all non-function words") is a content-word error rate, not a biasing-word rate, yet it sits beside M3AV BWER in Table 2, Figure 3, and the human study. EdX SRT captions are called "independently verified gold-standard" with no audit, though caption editing (dropped fillers, false starts) inflates WER for verbatim recognizers and may covary with instructor group. The unit of WER computation (pooled vs per-recording mean; capping) is never stated, which matters enormously for the looping Whisper-L runs.

### Minor and clarification requests

- One-sided tests are reported as "McNemar's exact test", which is conventionally two-sided; no multiple-comparison policy for 7+ tests; items and clips are nested in recordings/speakers with no clustering adjustment.
- Table 1 reports pre-exclusion counts (1,380) and "27 courses" vs the text's "27 instructors"; post-exclusion L1/L2 split never given; no per-reason breakdown of the 148 exclusions.
- "Whisper-M and Whisper-S achieve the strongest overall WER and BWER on both datasets" is false for M3AV BWER (Whisper-L-t 0.058 < Whisper-S 0.065); Whisper-L-t is "substantially worse" only on EdX (M3AV 0.081 vs 0.079).
- The "redundancy" mechanism offered for Humanities/Social Sciences robustness is asserted, never measured, and Science is equally robust.
- Insertion errors are excluded from the downstream analysis although they are the dominant Whisper-L failure mode; no S/D/I breakdown is given anywhere.
- "Additionally run with decoding language fixed to English" leaves it unclear which Whisper run populates Table 2 and the figures; the auto-detect vs fixed comparison would itself support the thesis.
- Which ASR system's mishearing is used by the superficial-cue filter, and how many items have a keyword error under each of the seven systems.
- Table 4 mixes proportions and percentage points; "n=173" (discordant pairs pooled over 900 model-item pairs) is undefined in the text.
- Naming inconsistencies (Wav2Vec2 / Wav2Vec / wav2vec2; GPT4o / gpt4o; Llama3 / Llama-3.1-8B / llama; "ref" vs "reference (gold)"); Table 7 typeset in a smaller font with variable names ("percent_L2").
- Keyword-priority criteria collapsed into a run-on paragraph with inline " - " separators; several garbled sentences (the LLM-judge triage sentence; "Deletions of these erroneous knowledge-intensive terms"; the Table 3 lead-in); McNemar is described as verifying that losses "concentrate in specific items", which it does not test.
- Related Work placed after Results and duplicating the Introduction; reference-list entries with "et al." author fields and missing venues (Hollands et al. 2022).
- The human-transcription BWER (0.28-0.34) vs Whisper-M (~0.05-0.07) implies hearing listeners recover far fewer technical terms from audio than the transcript contains; the paper should discuss what this means for the audio-vs-transcript baseline.

## 7. Strengths

- A clear, well-motivated problem: WER weights a mis-recognized enzyme name the same as a dropped "the", and educational access depends on the former. The Figure 1 example ("base excision repair" -> "basic session repair") communicates the failure mode instantly.
- Paired design on identical items with within-item discordance analysis, effect sizes next to p-values, and a format ablation (open-ended) that changes only the answer format. All reported test statistics reproduce from the reported counts.
- Real educational speech at scale (1,355 recordings, two corpora, 27+ instructors, 1,107 items), with L1/L2 labels assigned per speaker rather than guessed from accent, and unresolved speakers excluded rather than guessed.
- Generation from the reference rather than ASR output; generator excluded from answering; disclosed partial role overlap; deterministic decoding where supported; matched prompts across models.
- Keyword-level error analysis with substitution/deletion typing that links QA failures to the ASR mechanism, plus a human-vs-ASR comparison on the same clips with the same metric.
- Practically important observations, even if under-analysed: off-the-shelf larger Whisper checkpoints are worse on long lecture audio; substitution errors on technical terms dominate; multiple-choice probes can hide part of the effect.
- A candid Limitations section (QA LLMs are proxies, not disabled learners; visual channel ignored; listener-sample dependence; correlation is not causation).
- The domain-level claim that technical-vocabulary errors concentrate in Engineering, Computer Science, and NIH biomedical talks is supported by Figure 3 (Whisper-M BWER 0.074 / 0.072 / 0.061 vs 0.035-0.047 elsewhere), and for the wav2vec2 family BWER really does exceed WER (Wav2Vec2-B on M3AV: 0.402 vs 0.274), which is exactly the pattern the "WER hides term corruption" argument needs.
- The framework is model-agnostic and immediately usable: any ASR system or LLM can be swapped in, so an accessibility office could re-run it when procuring captioning vendors, and the L1/L2-labelled, question-enriched corpora would be a reusable resource if released.

## 9. Verification notes

Ninety-six findings (the methodology, statistics, consistency, ASR, and part of the LLM-QA lenses) were each re-examined by two independent adversarial verifiers. Outcomes that changed the text above:

- **Dropped:** the claimed rounding error in Table 4's Cohen's h for Llama3 (0.253 vs 0.252). The reported value is exact for the unrounded accuracies 232/300 and 198/300.
- **Narrowed:** the NIH/venue confound applies to the human-study clip sample, not to the Figure 2 corpus (both corpora are ~75% L2 by design; EdX is 91% of videos), so the Figure 2 confound is within-EdX subject/instructor, still never cross-tabulated.
- **Narrowed:** "M3AV labels partly rest on name origin" is not demonstrated; the paper can be read as excluding all unverified speakers. The verifiable defects are the unreported verified fraction, proxies that do not record native language, and the undisclosed down-selection rule.
- **Narrowed:** the "gap narrows for the strongest Whisper models" sentence is literally true in absolute terms for Whisper-S/M; only the relative reading and the "recent models reduce the gap" inference are unsupported.
- **Narrowed:** the open-ended comparison's larger net directional effect (3.2 -> 11.9 pt) cannot be produced by symmetric judge noise alone, so the format effect is real; what is not established is its size, given the unvalidated judge and the changed effective item set.
- **Narrowed:** "frequently 100% accurate" is imprecise rather than false (per-model accuracy in the imperfect clips is 0.84-0.90); whether clip questions were generated from ASR output is ambiguous, not stated.
- **Narrowed:** Cohen's h labels are generous/loose rather than wrong; one-sided testing is disclosed and changes no conclusion; the 148-exclusion passages are compatible; "Table 11" is presumably an appendix table rather than nonexistent; four (not five) results are deferred to the appendix.
- **Narrowed:** the verifier/answerer ordering (GPT4o > Llama3 > Qwen2.5) also holds on the full set, so the ordering is not evidence of circularity; the ceiling level and suppressed "away" counts on the verified subset are.
- **Narrowed:** human transcription BWER of 0.28-0.34 on 25-word jargon clips is plausible for lay listeners; the defect is the unequal-conditions comparison with full-recording ASR.
- **Confirmed unchanged:** the text-vs-figure accuracy contradiction; the pooled Table 3 N; BWER below WER for all Whisper cells; the flat ~1.4x relative L1/L2 ratio; the untested Table 7 (Fisher p = 0.20); the missing QA-by-topic and QA-by-L1/L2 results; the Whisper-L artifact reuse; the unidentified checkpoints and decoding settings; the undefined aggregation unit; the missing alignment procedure.
- **Not adversarially verified** (usage limit): the human-study, accessibility, novelty, ethics/reproducibility, writing, skeptic, and advocate lenses. Their numeric claims were recomputed here; their literature claims cite only well-known lines of work.
