# Rubric — NeurIPS Datasets & Benchmarks track

A dataset paper is judged on the artifact, not on the model trained with it. A
weak pilot in a strong dataset paper is a limitation; a strong pilot cannot
rescue an unvalidated dataset. Grade in this order.

## 1. Is the artifact real and obtainable?

- Is there a **URL** — anonymised at submission, permanent at camera-ready?
  The abstract saying "we release X" is not a release. Search the full text and
  record exactly what you find.
- Licence of the new artifact, and licences of every source it derives from.
  Does the source licence actually permit redistribution of the derived form?
- Is there a datasheet or dataset card, hosting plan, and a maintenance
  statement?
- Format, schema, and loading instructions sufficient to use it without the
  authors.

A dataset paper that cannot be checked against its dataset is not reviewable as a
dataset paper. Say so plainly rather than reviewing the prose instead.

## 2. Are the labels valid?

This is the centre of the review. For each annotation the artifact ships:

- **Provenance** — human-annotated, inherited from a source corpus, or produced
  by a heuristic the authors wrote?
- **Validation** — for anything algorithmically derived, what establishes that it
  is correct? Agreement with human annotation on a sample, with a reported rate,
  is the standard. Qualitative inspection by the authors is weaker; state that it
  is weaker.
- **Circularity** — is any label evaluated using the procedure that produced it?
  A rule classifier scored against ground truth generated by the same rule
  classifier measures self-consistency, not accuracy. This is a critical finding
  wherever it appears, and it is easy to miss because the numbers look ordinary.
- **Coverage** — what fraction lands in `other`, `unknown`, or a fallback bucket,
  and how does that fraction affect every downstream number?

Watch for **derived-quantity faithfulness**: a chain, caption, or rationale
generated from computed artifacts is faithful *to those artifacts*, which is a
weaker property than being correct, and the two are easy to conflate. If the
paper defines correctness as agreement-with-its-own-pipeline, then a claim of
"no violations found" is a tautology. Check whether the paper distinguishes
these; the good ones say so explicitly and the claim should be read in that light.

## 3. Does the task framing support the stated use?

- Does the label distribution contain what the motivating task needs? A
  detection dataset with no negative examples cannot train a detector to abstain,
  whatever its size.
- Are train/eval splits disjoint in the way that matters — by source image, by
  generator, by instruction style, not merely by row?
- Is the held-out set large enough and clean enough to support the comparisons
  drawn on it?

## 4. Baselines and comparison

- Is any existing method from the literature run on this artifact? A dataset
  paper without a baseline from prior work gives no way to calibrate difficulty.
- Are the reported numbers meaningfully above chance? Compute chance yourself
  given the class count and the metric.
- Single run, single configuration, single seed — is variance reported at all?

## 5. Statistical treatment

- Runs, seeds, error bars, significance tests on the claims that carry the paper.
- A confidence interval on an incidental measurement, with none on the headline
  result, is worse than none at all: it signals the authors know how and chose
  not to.
- Recompute every derived number. See `PIPELINE.md` Stage 3.

## 6. Novelty and positioning

- Is the novelty claim ("first dataset to…") narrow enough to be true, and is it
  tested against the closest prior work rather than against a strawman?
- Count citations that are *discussed* versus citations bundled into a bracket.
  A related-work section that lists nine papers in one citation group has not
  engaged with any of them.
- Separate the contribution from what is inherited. If the taxonomy, the images,
  and the instructions all come from a source corpus, name what was added.

## 7. Ethics, impact, safeguards

- Dual-use is discussed concretely, not as a paragraph of throat-clearing.
- Safeguards: gated release, licence terms, misuse mitigations. Note when the
  checklist's safeguards answer talks about something other than safeguards —
  licence compliance is a different question and answering it here means the
  question was not answered.
- Human subjects, consent, and PII in source imagery. Person-centric categories
  raise this whether or not the authors collected the images themselves.

## 8. Checklist audit

Read the NeurIPS checklist against the paper rather than against itself. Every
`[Yes]` whose justification is not supported by the body is a finding, and a
cluster of them speaks to how carefully the submission was prepared. Check in
particular: reproducibility, open access, experimental settings (learning rate,
batch size, seeds, steps), significance, compute (hardware and hours), licences,
new assets, safeguards.

## Rating

| Score | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 6 | Excellent — artifact is validated, obtainable, and clearly advances the field. |
| 5 | Good — solid artifact and validation; limitations are acknowledged and bounded. |
| 4 | Borderline accept — real contribution, correctable gaps, nothing central unsupported. |
| 3 | Borderline reject — promising artifact, but a core claim or validation is missing. |
| 2 | Reject — central claims unsupported, or the artifact is unavailable or unvalidated. |
| 1 | Strong reject — fundamental flaws in construction, evaluation, or integrity. |

Set the rating from the findings, then check it: name the specific thing that
would have to change to move it up one step. If you cannot, the rating is not
yet grounded.
